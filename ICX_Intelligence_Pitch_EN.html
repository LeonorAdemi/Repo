Every JOIN is a set operation across whole tables. Indexes help — but each join hands an intermediate result to the next one, and that result grows with your data.

    


    

      
Graph · the traversal visits

entities actually visited · unchanged

      
An entity holds direct references to its neighbours. A hop is following a pointer, not searching a table — so the cost follows the neighbourhood, not the database.

    

  


  


  

    

      
What actually drives cost

      
Not depth — breadth. Thirty narrow hops are cheap. Three hops that fan out across half your parts catalogue are not. Typed relationships prune at every step, and each entity is visited once, so a traversal explores the relevant subgraph rather than every possible path.

    

    

      
In fairness · where SQL wins

      
Ask „total spend across all suppliers last year“ and the relational database wins outright — that question touches everything, and set operations are exactly the right tool. The graph wins when you start from a known point and follow relationships outward. Impact analysis is always the second kind.

Appendix · Why It Cannot Invent Facts

  
The model does not remember your data.
It goes and looks.

  
„What if the AI makes something up?“ is the right question to ask. The answer is not a promise — it is the architecture. Step through what actually happens between the question and the answer, and watch what the model holds at each moment.

What the model holds

        

      

      

        
What it therefore cannot do

        

      

    

  


  

    

      
A language model on its own

      
Answers from its weights. Asked for a part number, it produces the most plausible-looking string. There is no difference, inside the model, between recalling and inventing — which is precisely why it sounds equally confident either way.

    

    

      
A model on your graph

      
Answers from a traversal. It never produces a part number — it produces a query, and the graph produces the part number, together with the system and record it came from. The model phrases; it does not know.

    

  


  

    The structural point: the model is never the source of facts, so it cannot be the source of wrong ones. Every entity in an answer exists because a stored relationship was followed to reach it — and that path is the audit trail. This is the same mechanism as the evidence chain on the security slide, seen from the other side.
  


  

    What this does not claim: the model can still summarise clumsily, or a query can be phrased too narrowly and miss something — which is why every answer shows its path, so a human can see what was followed. And if your source system holds the wrong revision, the answer inherits it. Grounding does not make the model truthful. It moves the question of trust from the model back to your systems — where you can actually audit it.
  


  
A wrong answer you can trace is a data problem.
A wrong answer you cannot trace is an unusable system.
